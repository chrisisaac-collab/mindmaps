Index Funds — The Only Framework You Need
▼
Investing Has Been Solved
▼
Felix's core thesis is simple: use low-cost global index funds, capture market returns, and stop there. Academic literature consistently shows the vast majority of active fund managers underperform the index over the long run after fees. The solution is not complicated — the hard part is the psychology of actually sticking with it when markets get volatile and narratives turn scary.
The Case for Knowing Just Enough
▼
Counterintuitively, people who know a little — just enough to have conviction in index funds — outperform people who know a lot. More knowledge often leads to over-tinkering, stock-picking, and trying clever strategies that underperform. The person who buys a global index fund and ignores it beats the person who thinks they can outsmart the market.
Your First $10,000 — Three Steps
▼
Step one: build a small emergency fund covering 3–6 months of expenses. Step two: pay off high-interest debt — anything costing more than 6–7% is a guaranteed negative return on investment. Step three: invest the remainder in a low-cost global index fund and automate contributions so psychology cannot get in the way of execution.
Don't Look at Your Portfolio
▼
Academic research shows that more frequent portfolio monitoring leads to less risk-taking and lower returns. Daily fluctuations trigger loss aversion — the pain of a 5% drop outweighs the pleasure of a 6% gain. Investors who check quarterly or annually make far better decisions than those who monitor daily, because they experience smooth trends rather than terrifying noise.
Asset Allocation and Time Horizon
▼
How much to hold in stocks versus bonds depends on your time horizon and ability to tolerate volatility without panic-selling. Young investors with decades ahead can hold almost entirely equities — downturns allow them to buy more cheaply. Those nearing retirement need more stability to avoid sequence-of-returns risk, where a market crash early in retirement permanently impairs withdrawals.
🏠
Renting vs Buying — The 5% Rule
▼
The Hidden Unrecoverable Costs of Owning
▼
Homeowners systematically undercount the true cost of ownership. Property taxes (approximately 1% per year), maintenance (approximately 1% — likely more for older properties), and cost of capital (approximately 3% — either mortgage interest or the opportunity cost of equity tied up in the home) total roughly 5% of the home's value annually, none of which builds equity.
Applying the 5% Rule
▼
Multiply the home's purchase price by 5%, then divide by 12. This gives the break-even monthly rent. If you can rent an equivalent property for less, renting is the better financial decision. Example: a $300,000 home has a break-even rent of $1,250/month. Rent a comparable home for less and invest the difference — you come out ahead financially over the long run.
Renting Is Not Throwing Money Away
▼
The cultural belief that "rent is dead money" is financially illiterate. A renter who invests the down payment and monthly cost difference into a global index fund will in many markets end up with more wealth than the homeowner after 25 years. The renter is not throwing money away — they are paying for housing, exactly as the homeowner pays for unrecoverable costs.
When Buying Does Make Sense
▼
Buying makes financial sense when monthly ownership costs compare favourably to equivalent rent, when you plan to stay for 5+ years to amortise transaction costs, and when you have strong job security. Non-financial benefits — stability, the ability to renovate, pets, community — are real and worth valuing, but should be understood as lifestyle benefits, not financial returns.

Human Capital — Your Most Valuable Asset
▼
Under-Earning Is the #1 Financial Mistake
▼
Felix's most controversial point: treating income as fixed is the biggest financial error most people make. Academic data shows a strong mechanical relationship between investment in human capital and lifetime earnings. Formal education, trade skills, and entrepreneurship all increase your most important financial asset — yourself. The biggest lever is almost always on the income side, not the expenses side.
Rare and Complementary Skill Stacks
▼
The highest earnings come not from deepening a single skill but from combining rare and complementary skills the market values uniquely. Felix himself combined finance expertise with accessible content creation — making him one of a tiny number of finance professionals who can communicate academic research to millions. A biotech writer earns five times a general writer's rate simply by adding domain expertise to an existing writing skill.
Young People May Not Need to Save as Much
▼
Academic lifecycle consumption research suggests young people with low current but high expected future earnings may be suboptimal to save aggressively now. The principle: smooth consumption across your lifetime, saving more when you earn more. The critical caveat is that this requires actually increasing savings as income grows — which many people fail to do, ending up in trouble at 50.
Knowledge and Skills Are Indestructible
▼
Bartlett's five-bucket framework: knowledge, skills, resources, network, and reputation. Career earthquakes can drain the last three, but cannot take away what you know and can do. Early career focus should be entirely on filling the knowledge and skills buckets as deeply as possible — they compound quietly and cannot be taken away.

The Psychology of Money
▼
Your Brain Was Not Designed for Investing
▼
The human brain evolved for immediate survival, not for abstract long-term wealth accumulation. This creates systematic biases — loss aversion, present bias, recency bias — that cause people to sell at market bottoms, chase recent winners, and under-save for retirement. Understanding that these impulses are biological, not personal failures, is the first step to building systems that work around them.
The PERMA Model and What Wealth Is For
▼
Felix references the PERMA model from positive psychology — Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment — as a framework for understanding what wealth is actually for. Money is a means to these ends, not an end in itself. Understanding which ends matter most to you shapes how much you need to save, how much risk to take, and how to avoid accumulating wealth with no clear purpose.
Tightwads, Spendthrifts and Relationships
▼
Research shows tightwads (chronic under-spenders) and spendthrifts (chronic over-spenders) are attracted to each other but end up in conflict. Tightwads fail to convert wealth into wellbeing; spendthrifts fail to build any. Understanding your own money personality — and your partner's — before combining finances prevents one of the most reliable sources of both financial and relationship damage.
Never Decide from Financial Urgency
▼
Financial urgency — the panic of feeling "behind" — reliably produces the worst decisions: speculative FOMO investing at market peaks, panic-selling at bottoms, taking on debt to chase returns. Felix argues that when you feel financial urgency, that is precisely the moment to slow down, revert to your pre-set systematic framework, and do nothing until the emotion passes.

Top Financial Mistakes
▼
Products That Sound Good but Aren't
▼
The financial industry is structured around selling products, not providing advice. Actively managed funds, whole life insurance as an investment vehicle, structured products, and certain annuities consistently underperform simpler alternatives after fees. Felix's engineering test: what does the peer-reviewed academic evidence say about this product's expected net-of-fee return relative to an index fund?
Underestimating Catastrophic Risk
▼
Most financial plans account for expected scenarios but ignore tail risks — job loss, serious illness, divorce, long-term care. Felix argues for adequate insurance (disability, life, liability) as a financial foundation before optimising investments. Catastrophic risks that can wipe out decades of savings are systematically underpriced by individuals because they feel abstract and unlikely until they happen.
Crypto and Speculation
▼
Felix takes a measured academic view: assets without earnings, dividends, or a clear mechanism for value creation are speculative. That doesn't mean crypto can't go up, but it means there is no principled way to value it or size it in a portfolio. FOMO-driven allocation to speculative assets at market peaks is one of the most reliable wealth destroyers across history.
Holding Too Much Cash
▼
Cash feels safe but loses value in real terms every year through inflation. Many people hold far more cash than their emergency fund requires because comfort feels like security. For any money not needed within 2–3 years, academic evidence is clear: being out of the market has a very high expected cost in forgone returns over a multi-decade investing horizon.

AI, War and the Future of Wealth
▼
Should You Invest in AI?
▼
A global index fund already owns AI companies proportional to their market weight. Trying to overweight AI is a bet that you know more than every sophisticated institutional investor in the world — a high bar to clear. The academic evidence for most individual sector bets versus the broad index is not encouraging. If AI takes over the economy, your index fund will reflect it automatically.
AI Displacement and Human Capital
▼
Felix acknowledges AI displacement as a real economic force, particularly for routine cognitive tasks. His response connects back to human capital: the skills that remain valuable are those requiring creative combination, relationship-building, and judgment in novel situations — precisely the rare and complementary skill stacks he recommends building. Investing in yourself is the best hedge against AI disruption.
How War Changes Investing
▼
Geopolitical conflict creates volatility and narrative-driven market moves, but historical data shows that even major wars have not derailed long-run global equity returns for diversified investors. Geographic diversification — not concentrating in any single country — is the key protection. A global index fund provides this automatically without requiring any prediction of which conflicts will escalate.
Remortgage or Invest?
▼
This is a mathematical question that depends on your mortgage rate versus expected equity returns. At high rates (above 5–6%), paying down mortgage debt offers a guaranteed risk-free return equal to the rate — hard to beat. At low rates, equities have a higher expected return, but with volatility. Model your specific numbers rather than applying a blanket cultural rule either way.

Top Financial Mistakes

Felix's most common mistakes span both product selection and behaviour. On products: actively managed funds, complex structured products, and crypto speculation consistently fail to deliver risk-adjusted returns that justify their costs relative to simple index alternatives. The financial industry's incentive structure — advisers rewarded for selling rather than advising — means retail investors are systematically steered toward products that benefit the seller.

01

Use a global index fund and stop there

Pick a low-cost global index fund, set up automatic contributions, and resist the urge to tinker. Knowing just enough to stay the course beats knowing enough to hurt yourself with cleverness.

02

Apply the 5% rule before buying a home

Multiply the purchase price by 5% and divide by 12. If you can rent an equivalent property for less than this figure, renting and investing the difference is likely the superior financial decision.

03

Check your portfolio quarterly, not daily

Academic research confirms frequent portfolio monitoring leads to lower risk-taking and lower returns. Set a calendar reminder for quarterly reviews and ignore everything in between.

04

Build a rare and complementary skill stack

Adding one rare skill to an existing expertise can multiply earning power far more than deepening the existing skill. Identify what combination would make you one of a handful of people globally with that pairing.

05

Emergency fund first, then invest

Three to six months of expenses in cash before any investment. Without this buffer, the first financial shock forces you to sell at the worst possible time, turning temporary volatility into permanent losses.

06

Pay off high-interest debt before investing

Any debt costing more than 6–7% per year is a guaranteed negative return that no investment can reliably beat on a risk-adjusted basis. Clear it before directing money to the stock market.

07

Know your money personality and your partner's

Discuss tightwad vs spendthrift tendencies explicitly before combining finances. Misalignment here is one of the most reliable sources of both financial damage and relationship conflict.

08

Insure against catastrophe before optimising returns

Disability insurance, adequate life cover, and liability protection should be in place before focusing on investment returns. One uninsured catastrophic event can destroy decades of careful wealth accumulation.

09

Never make financial decisions from urgency

Financial urgency reliably produces the worst decisions. When you feel panic about money, slow down, revert to your pre-set framework, and do nothing until the emotion passes.

10

Ask "what does the evidence say?" about every product

Before buying any financial product — fund, insurance wrapper, crypto, structured note — ask what peer-reviewed academic research says about its expected net-of-fee return versus a simple index fund. The evidence will usually save you.
