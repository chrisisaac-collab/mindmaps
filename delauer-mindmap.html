🧬 Collagen: What It Is & Why It Declines ▼
The structural protein of youth ▾
Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body, forming the structural scaffold of skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage, and bone. In skin specifically, Type I collagen provides tensile strength and elasticity. When collagen fibres are dense and well-organised, skin appears firm, plump, and smooth. As they degrade and thin, wrinkles, sagging, and dullness follow inevitably.
The production cliff after 25 ▾
Collagen production peaks in early adulthood and declines by approximately 1–1.5% per year from age 25. By 40, many people have lost 10–15% of their original collagen density. By 50, the loss is accelerating further. This is why skin changes that seem sudden in middle age actually represent decades of slow, cumulative decline that becomes visible only after crossing a threshold.
Collagenase: the enzyme breaking you down ▾
Collagenase enzymes — particularly matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) — are upregulated by UV radiation, cortisol (chronic stress), advanced glycation end-products (from high-sugar diets), smoking, and environmental pollution. These enzymes actively degrade existing collagen fibres. Slowing collagenase activity is just as important as stimulating new collagen production.
Glycation: sugar's war on your skin ▾
Excess blood glucose attaches to collagen fibres through a process called glycation, forming advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). Glycated collagen is stiff, cross-linked, and loses its normal springy architecture. It also becomes more resistant to normal turnover, meaning damaged fibres accumulate rather than being replaced. High-glycaemic diets are a primary driver of accelerated skin ageing.
💊 Collagen Supplementation — The Evidence ▼
Does oral collagen actually work? ▾
Scepticism about oral collagen stems from the assumption that digestion simply breaks it into amino acids indistinguishable from any other protein. However, research shows that hydrolysed collagen peptides — particularly di- and tri-peptides — are absorbed intact through the gut lining and accumulate in skin tissue, where they act as direct building blocks and also stimulate fibroblast activity to produce new collagen.
Clinical evidence for skin outcomes ▾
Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled trials show statistically significant improvements in skin elasticity, hydration, and wrinkle depth after 8–12 weeks of daily collagen peptide supplementation (typically 5–10g/day). Effects are most pronounced in older participants whose baseline collagen density is lower, suggesting supplementation provides the greatest benefit to those who need it most.
Type matters: Type I and Type III ▾
Type I collagen (bovine or marine-sourced) is the dominant structural protein of skin and the primary target for anti-ageing supplementation. Type III collagen, which co-localises with Type I in skin, also declines with age. High-quality supplements contain both. Marine collagen is derived from fish skin and scales, has a lower molecular weight than bovine collagen, and may have marginally superior bioavailability.
Optimal dosing and timing ▾
Research consistently supports 5–10g of hydrolysed collagen peptides per day. Timing is secondary to consistency, but taking collagen with Vitamin C (which is a cofactor in the collagen synthesis enzyme prolyl hydroxylase) meaningfully enhances conversion into structural collagen. A daily morning routine — one scoop in coffee or a smoothie with citrus — is a practical and evidence-supported protocol.
🔆 Vitamin C: The Collagen Co-Factor ▼
Why Vitamin C is non-negotiable ▾
Collagen synthesis is biochemically impossible without Vitamin C. The enzyme prolyl hydroxylase, which stabilises the collagen triple-helix structure, requires Vitamin C as an essential cofactor. Without adequate Vitamin C, newly synthesised collagen fibres are structurally weak and break down rapidly. Scurvy — extreme Vitamin C deficiency — is fundamentally a collagen synthesis failure disease.
Antioxidant protection of existing collagen ▾
Beyond its role in synthesis, Vitamin C is a potent antioxidant that neutralises reactive oxygen species (free radicals) — the primary molecular mechanism through which UV radiation, pollution, and metabolic stress degrade existing collagen. Adequate dietary Vitamin C creates a protective buffer around skin tissue, slowing the rate at which collagenase enzymes are activated.
Dietary sources vs supplements ▾
Vitamin C is readily available from whole food sources — bell peppers, kiwi, strawberries, citrus, broccoli, and kale all provide abundant amounts. For most people, a diet genuinely rich in fresh vegetables and fruit provides sufficient Vitamin C for collagen synthesis. Supplementation (500–1000mg/day) is a practical fallback for those whose diet is inconsistent, but food sources remain the primary recommendation.
⏱️ Biological Age vs Chronological Age ▼
What biological age actually measures ▾
Biological age is determined not by years lived but by the accumulated cellular and molecular damage in the body. Key markers include telomere length (caps on chromosomes that shorten with each cell division), epigenetic methylation patterns (Horvath's DNAm clock), inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-6), and mitochondrial efficiency. A person can be chronologically 45 and biologically 55 — or vice versa — depending on their lifestyle history.
Skin as a biological age indicator ▾
Skin collagen density and quality is increasingly being used as a proxy measure of systemic biological ageing. The same processes that degrade skin collagen — oxidative stress, inflammation, glycation, and mitochondrial dysfunction — are occurring throughout the body simultaneously. Skin changes are visible evidence of systemic ageing that is also happening in blood vessels, joints, the brain, and organs.
Can biological age be reversed? ▾
Emerging research — including studies using epigenetic clocks — demonstrates that biological age can be measurably reduced through dietary intervention, exercise, sleep optimisation, and stress reduction. Studies by Morgan Levine and David Sinclair show meaningful reductions in epigenetic age scores after lifestyle interventions. Collagen peptide supplementation, within a broader anti-ageing protocol, contributes to this reversal particularly at the skin and connective tissue level.
NAD+ and mitochondrial ageing ▾
NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme essential for mitochondrial energy production and DNA repair. NAD+ levels decline precipitously with age, impairing cellular energy metabolism and reducing the activity of sirtuins — longevity-associated proteins that regulate gene expression, DNA repair, and stress resistance. Precursors such as NMN and NR can partially restore NAD+ levels, and their interaction with collagen metabolism is an active area of research.
☀️ UV, Inflammation & Environmental Ageing ▼
UV radiation: the dominant external ager ▾
Approximately 80% of visible facial ageing is attributable to UV exposure (photoageing), not chronological age. UV radiation directly damages collagen fibres, activates MMP collagenase enzymes, generates reactive oxygen species, and causes cumulative DNA damage in skin cells. Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30+ — even on cloudy days — is the single highest-leverage topical intervention for preserving skin collagen.
Chronic inflammation as internal photoageing ▾
Inflammageing — the chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation that increases with age — accelerates collagen degradation through the same MMP pathways activated by UV. Poor diet (particularly UPF-heavy and high-glycaemic), inadequate sleep, chronic psychological stress, and lack of exercise all elevate inflammatory markers. In this sense, lifestyle-driven inflammation is internal photoageing occurring around the clock.
Pollution and skin ageing ▾
Particulate matter (PM2.5) and ground-level ozone penetrate the skin barrier, generate free radicals, and activate inflammatory pathways that degrade collagen. Research from urban populations shows that air pollution independently accelerates skin ageing beyond UV exposure alone. Antioxidant-rich nutrition provides partial systemic protection; physical barriers (clothing, avoiding peak pollution times) offer additional defence.
🥗 Diet & Lifestyle Protocol for Skin Longevity ▼
The anti-glycation diet ▾
Minimising dietary AGE formation requires reducing high-glycaemic foods (white sugar, refined carbohydrates, high-fructose corn syrup) and — perhaps counterintuitively — favouring low-heat cooking methods. AGEs form most prolifically through high-heat dry cooking (grilling, frying, roasting). Steaming, poaching, and slow-cooking at lower temperatures significantly reduce dietary AGE load without requiring elimination of otherwise healthy foods.
Protein adequacy for collagen synthesis ▾
Collagen is a protein, and producing it requires adequate dietary protein overall. Glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline are the dominant amino acids in collagen. While the body can synthesise glycine endogenously, production may be insufficient for optimal collagen synthesis — giving dietary sources (bone broth, gelatin, collagen peptide supplements, and glycine-rich foods) a useful complement to general protein intake.
Hydration, sleep, and stress — the underrated triad ▾
Skin hydration is maintained by hyaluronic acid in the dermis, which itself requires adequate water intake and is impaired by chronic cortisol elevation. Sleep is when growth hormone is secreted, driving cellular repair including collagen synthesis — 7–9 hours is not cosmetically optional. Chronic psychological stress elevates cortisol, which directly suppresses collagen synthesis and upregulates MMP collagenase activity. Managing stress is skin care.
Exercise and collagen remodelling ▾
Resistance training and moderate aerobic exercise stimulate fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis, both in skin and connective tissue. Studies on exercise timing show that consuming collagen peptides with Vitamin C approximately one hour before exercise further amplifies collagen synthesis in the targeted tissue — a practically usable protocol for those training regularly who want to compound the benefit.

Collagen: Structure, Decline, and Why It Matters

Thomas De Lauer opens with a foundational explanation of collagen's role as the body's primary structural protein. In skin, Type I collagen provides tensile strength and elasticity; when its fibres are dense and well-organised, skin looks firm and smooth. Production peaks in early adulthood and declines by approximately 1–1.5% per year from around age 25. By middle age, this slow attrition becomes visibly apparent in the form of wrinkles, laxity, and dullness. The mechanism of decline is not simply passive — collagenase enzymes (matrix metalloproteinases, or MMPs) are actively upregulated by UV radiation, sugar-driven glycation, chronic stress, and environmental pollution, and they accelerate degradation of the collagen that remains.

The Science Behind Collagen Supplementation

De Lauer addresses the most common objection to collagen supplementation — that digestion simply breaks collagen into generic amino acids — with the nuance the research warrants. Hydrolysed collagen peptides, specifically, are absorbed partially intact as di- and tri-peptides that cross the gut wall and accumulate in skin tissue. There, they both provide structural building blocks and signal fibroblasts (skin's collagen-producing cells) to increase synthesis. Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials support improvements in skin elasticity, hydration, and wrinkle depth after 8–12 weeks of daily supplementation at 5–10g. Effects are most pronounced in older participants whose collagen baseline is lower — exactly the demographic for whom supplementation makes the most intuitive sense.

Vitamin C: The Non-Negotiable Co-Factor

The episode gives considerable attention to Vitamin C, which is biochemically required for collagen synthesis — not as an optional enhancement but as an enzymatic necessity. Prolyl hydroxylase, the enzyme that stabilises the triple-helix structure of new collagen, cannot function without Vitamin C as a cofactor. Additionally, Vitamin C's role as a free-radical neutraliser provides direct protection of existing collagen from oxidative damage. De Lauer's practical takeaway is simple: take collagen peptides alongside a Vitamin C source, whether that's a squeeze of citrus or a supplement — the combination meaningfully outperforms collagen alone.

Biological Age, Epigenetics, and Skin as a Window

The episode positions skin collagen not merely as a cosmetic concern but as a visible indicator of systemic biological ageing. The same processes degrading skin — oxidative stress, inflammation, glycation, and mitochondrial dysfunction — are occurring simultaneously in blood vessels, cartilage, the brain, and other organs. De Lauer discusses epigenetic clocks (particularly Horvath's DNAm clock) as tools for measuring biological versus chronological age, noting that lifestyle interventions including diet, sleep, and exercise have been shown in clinical research to measurably reduce biological age scores. NAD+ depletion is introduced as a parallel ageing mechanism — declining NAD+ levels impair mitochondrial energy production and sirtuin activity, and precursors such as NMN and NR represent a related intervention space.

UV, Inflammation, and Environmental Ageing

Photoageing — collagen destruction driven by UV radiation — is responsible for approximately 80% of visible facial ageing, making daily SPF use the single most evidence-backed topical intervention available. De Lauer extends this framework to what he terms "internal photoageing": chronic low-grade inflammation driven by poor diet, sleep deprivation, stress, and inactivity activates the same MMP collagenase pathways as UV radiation, degrading collagen from within rather than from without. Air pollution compounds the picture, with particulate matter independently shown to accelerate skin ageing through free-radical generation.

Practical Protocol: Diet, Lifestyle, and Supplementation

The episode closes with a synthesised protocol: reduce dietary AGE formation by minimising refined sugars and favouring lower-heat cooking methods; ensure adequate protein intake with attention to glycine-rich sources; pair collagen supplementation with Vitamin C and time it before resistance training for amplified effect; prioritise 7–9 hours of sleep (when growth hormone drives cellular repair); manage psychological stress to reduce cortisol-driven collagen suppression; and wear broad-spectrum SPF daily without exception. Together, these interventions target collagen synthesis, protection of existing collagen, and reduction of the biological ageing processes that accelerate skin — and systemic — decline.

TAKEAWAY 01

Take 5–10g of hydrolysed collagen peptides (Type I, from bovine or marine sources) daily. Consistency over 8–12 weeks is required for measurable skin improvements — don't judge efficacy before that window.

TAKEAWAY 02

Always pair collagen with Vitamin C. Vitamin C is a biochemical requirement for collagen synthesis, not an optional add-on. A squeeze of lemon or lime in your collagen drink is sufficient; a 500mg supplement works too.

TAKEAWAY 03

Wear SPF 30+ every day — including overcast days and when working near windows. Approximately 80% of visible facial ageing comes from UV exposure, making daily sunscreen the single highest-leverage skin intervention available.

TAKEAWAY 04

Reduce refined sugar and high-glycaemic foods. Dietary sugar glycates collagen fibres, making them stiff and damaged. This is a structural change, not just a metabolic one — it directly creates wrinkles and dullness.

TAKEAWAY 05

Favour lower-heat cooking methods (steaming, poaching, slow-cooking) where possible. High-heat dry cooking (grilling, frying, roasting) dramatically increases dietary AGE intake, which accelerates collagen cross-linking and degradation.

TAKEAWAY 06

Time your collagen supplement approximately 1 hour before resistance training. Research shows this timing amplifies collagen synthesis in musculoskeletal tissue — a compounding benefit for those already exercising regularly.

TAKEAWAY 07

Prioritise 7–9 hours of sleep. Growth hormone — the body's primary cellular repair signal — is secreted predominantly during deep sleep. Consistently short sleep suppresses collagen repair at the biological level.

TAKEAWAY 08

Treat stress management as part of your skin care routine. Cortisol directly suppresses collagen synthesis and activates MMP enzymes that degrade existing collagen. Chronic unmanaged stress is biologically equivalent to an accelerant on skin ageing.

TAKEAWAY 09

Eat a Vitamin C-rich diet — bell peppers, kiwi, strawberries, broccoli, and citrus are all excellent sources. Food-first beats supplementation for a reason: the full antioxidant matrix in whole foods provides broader collagen protection than isolated supplements.

TAKEAWAY 10

Consider getting a biological age test (epigenetic clock via a service like TruDiagnostic or Elysium) as a baseline. It gives you a measurable target for lifestyle interventions — seeing your biological age improve is a powerful motivational anchor for sustaining the habits that got you there.
