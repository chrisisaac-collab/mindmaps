⚡ Takeaways
Rethinking Carbohydrates
🚫 The Carb Myths We've Believed
▼
Carbs make you fat
The belief that carbs uniquely cause fat storage is a conflation of insulin's role with overall energy balance. Research consistently shows that when calories and protein are matched, low-carb and high-carb diets produce equivalent fat loss outcomes. The real driver is sustained caloric surplus, not carbohydrate intake alone.
Insulin from carbs traps fat
The "carbohydrate-insulin model" in its extreme form has been largely disproven. Insulin is a storage and signalling hormone that also drives nutrients into muscle. Chronically elevated insulin from overeating is the problem — not the transient insulin spike from a single carbohydrate meal in a healthy, active individual.
All carbs are the same
Grouping white bread with sweet potato or lentils under one "carbs are bad" label ignores fibre content, glycaemic index, nutrient density, and fermentation potential. The source and matrix of carbohydrates radically changes how the body processes and responds to them.
Early weight loss = fat loss on low-carb
The rapid 2–4 kg drop in the first week of low-carb dieting is almost entirely glycogen depletion and associated water loss — roughly 3–4 g of water per gram of stored glycogen. This is physiologically meaningful for some contexts but is not fat loss and should not be interpreted as superior fat-burning.
Carbs cause inflammation
Refined carbohydrates — particularly ultra-processed foods high in fructose and seed oils — are pro-inflammatory. But whole-food carbohydrates rich in fibre, polyphenols, and resistant starch actively support anti-inflammatory pathways and feed beneficial gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids.
🧬 What Carbs Actually Do
▼
Glucose as primary fuel
The brain consumes roughly 120 g of glucose per day and has a strong preference for it — particularly in high-demand cognitive states. Red blood cells and parts of the kidney medulla are obligate glucose consumers. The body will manufacture glucose via gluconeogenesis if dietary intake is absent, which itself has metabolic costs.
Muscle glycogen and performance
Intramuscular glycogen is the dominant substrate for high-intensity effort above ~70% VO2 max. Depleted glycogen stores impair force production, reduce power output, and compromise recovery. For active individuals and athletes, adequate carbohydrate intake is not optional — it is a performance prerequisite.
Thyroid and hormonal impact
Prolonged carbohydrate restriction suppresses T3 (active thyroid hormone) production and can elevate cortisol and reduce testosterone — particularly in individuals who are already lean or under training stress. Adequate glucose availability supports the conversion of T4 to T3 and maintains a healthier hormonal environment for body recomposition.
Gut microbiome and fibre
Fermentable fibres — inulin, pectin, resistant starch — are technically carbohydrates that feed Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species. Their fermentation yields butyrate, propionate, and acetate: short-chain fatty acids that strengthen the gut lining, regulate appetite, and exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects across the gut-brain axis.
Insulin's full role
Insulin is not simply a "fat storage hormone." It is anabolic for muscle, anti-catabolic (preventing muscle breakdown), and critical for nutrient partitioning. The problem is not insulin per se but chronically elevated baseline insulin driven by sedentary behaviour, excess body fat, and poor diet quality — not by carbohydrate timing in an active person.
⏱ Carb Timing & Context
▼
Post-workout carb window
The post-exercise period — roughly 30–90 minutes after resistance training — represents a unique window of elevated GLUT4 translocation to muscle cell membranes. During this period, glucose is taken up by muscle independently of insulin, making it an ideal time to replenish glycogen without promoting fat storage. Combining fast-absorbing carbs with protein amplifies muscle protein synthesis.
Morning carb sensitivity
Cortisol is naturally elevated in the morning, which can temporarily reduce insulin sensitivity. For most people, front-loading carbohydrates early in the day — particularly for active individuals — takes advantage of higher insulin sensitivity windows following morning training or fasted movement, and aligns glucose disposal with periods of higher metabolic rate.
Evening carbs and sleep
Contrary to popular belief, consuming moderate carbohydrates in the evening is not inherently fattening. Some research shows that evening carbohydrates can actually support sleep quality by stimulating serotonin and melatonin precursor production. The key variable is total daily intake and overall energy balance — not which meal the carbs appear in.
Carbs around high-intensity training
Strategic carbohydrate placement around high-intensity work — both as pre-training fuel and post-training recovery — is supported by extensive exercise physiology research. This targeted approach allows individuals to maintain lower baseline insulin while still providing glucose when the body is primed to use and store it in muscle rather than adipose tissue.
Carb cycling as a middle path
Carb cycling — alternating between higher-carb training days and lower-carb rest or light days — represents a pragmatic approach that captures benefits of both ketogenic and higher-carb protocols. On low-carb days, fat oxidation and insulin sensitivity improve; on high-carb days, glycogen is replenished and anabolic signalling is amplified. DeLauer has long advocated this as his preferred long-term model.
🌾 Carb Quality Matters Most
▼
Whole food carbs vs. processed carbs
Sweet potato, oats, legumes, fruit, and root vegetables are categorically different from white bread, cookies, and breakfast cereals despite similar macronutrient profiles. Whole-food carbs arrive packaged with fibre, polyphenols, vitamins, minerals, and water — all of which slow digestion, blunt the glucose response, and deliver micronutrient value that processed carbs lack entirely.
Resistant starch benefits
Resistant starch — found in cooked and cooled rice, unripe bananas, cooked lentils, and potato starch — passes through the small intestine largely intact and ferments in the colon. This produces butyrate, improves insulin sensitivity, and lowers post-meal blood glucose. Cooling cooked starches before eating meaningfully increases resistant starch content.
Fructose vs. glucose metabolism
Fructose is processed almost entirely by the liver and does not trigger insulin or leptin signalling in the same way as glucose — blunting satiety cues and potentially increasing liver fat accumulation at high doses. However, the fructose in whole fruit (paired with fibre, water, and polyphenols) is metabolically distinct from high-fructose corn syrup in processed foods.
The food matrix effect
How a food is structured — its "food matrix" — determines its glycaemic impact as much as its sugar content. Whole almonds and almond flour have identical macros but very different glucose responses. The physical structure of food slows enzymatic access, reducing peak glucose and insulin responses. Processing destroys this matrix, which is why ultra-processed foods are metabolically damaging regardless of their listed carbohydrate grams.
Polyphenols and carb interaction
Polyphenols in berries, dark chocolate, and colourful vegetables actively inhibit glucose absorption, improve insulin signalling at the cellular level, and support AMPK activation — a cellular energy sensor that mimics aspects of fasting and exercise. Eating polyphenol-rich foods alongside carbohydrates meaningfully changes their metabolic impact.
🔥 Metabolic Flexibility
▼
What metabolic flexibility actually means
A metabolically flexible individual efficiently oxidises fat when fasted or resting, and seamlessly switches to glucose oxidation when carbohydrates are available or exercise intensity rises. Metabolic inflexibility — the inability to switch — is a hallmark of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. The goal is not to avoid carbs permanently but to preserve the ability to use both fuels well.
How strict low-carb can backfire
Prolonged carbohydrate avoidance can paradoxically impair glucose tolerance when carbs are eventually reintroduced — a phenomenon called physiological insulin resistance. The muscle and liver adapt to fat oxidation and become temporarily less efficient at glucose disposal. This is distinct from pathological insulin resistance but is worth understanding for anyone periodically reintroducing carbohydrates.
Building flexibility through training
Aerobic exercise, particularly Zone 2 training at moderate intensity, is the most powerful tool for improving fat oxidation capacity and overall metabolic flexibility. Resistance training improves glucose disposal into muscle. The combination of both — plus appropriate carbohydrate intake — builds a metabolism that handles glucose efficiently without requiring chronic restriction.
Context determines carb appropriateness
A sedentary, insulin-resistant individual with excess visceral fat may legitimately benefit from significant carbohydrate restriction. A lean, active person training 4–5 days per week who restricts carbs aggressively is potentially compromising performance, hormonal health, and recovery without proportional benefit. Carb needs are deeply individual and context-dependent — not universally high or universally low.
DeLauer's evolved position
DeLauer has publicly moved away from dogmatic keto advocacy towards a nuanced position emphasising metabolic flexibility, carb quality, and strategic timing. He now frames carbohydrates not as inherently harmful but as a tool that can be deployed or reduced based on individual metabolic health, activity level, and goals — a significant evolution from his earlier strict low-carb messaging.
✅ Practical Application
▼
Best carb sources to prioritise
DeLauer consistently highlights: sweet potato, white rice (post-workout), oats, legumes, berries, and cooked-then-cooled starches as top choices. These provide fibre, resistant starch, polyphenols, and micronutrients while having a manageable glucose response. He avoids bread, pasta, and processed grains as default daily sources.
How to test your own carb tolerance
Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) tools like Dexcom or Libre allow individuals to see real-time glucose responses to specific foods. This personal metabolic data is more instructive than population-level glycaemic index tables, since two people can have profoundly different glucose responses to the same food based on gut microbiome, activity history, and metabolic health.
Pairing strategies to blunt glucose spikes
Consuming carbohydrates after protein and fat, or alongside vinegar, cinnamon, or fibre-rich vegetables, meaningfully slows gastric emptying and reduces peak glucose response. Walking 10–15 minutes after a carbohydrate-containing meal accelerates glucose clearance into muscle via GLUT4 activation, lowering the insulin load required to restore baseline blood glucose.
Reintroducing carbs after low-carb periods
After extended low-carb dieting, carbohydrate reintroduction should be gradual and timed around exercise to avoid the exaggerated glucose response that comes with temporary physiological insulin resistance. Starting with root vegetables and legumes rather than refined grains, and combining with resistance training, allows the body to restore normal glucose handling capacity over 1–3 weeks.
Protein, fibre, and carb sequencing
Research shows that eating protein and fibre first, then carbohydrates at a meal, reduces post-meal glucose by up to 30% compared to eating carbs first. This "food order" effect is simple, cost-free, and requires no dietary restriction — just a change in sequencing. It works by slowing gastric emptying and pre-stimulating incretin hormone secretion before glucose enters the bloodstream.

The Carb Myths We've Inherited

For the past two decades, carbohydrates have been cast as the primary villain in modern metabolic disease — blamed for obesity, diabetes, inflammation, and cognitive decline. This narrative, amplified by the rise of ketogenic dieting, created a binary thinking problem: carbs are bad, fat is good. DeLauer's central argument in this video is that this framing is fundamentally wrong, not because carbohydrates are benign, but because the blanket condemnation ignores biology, context, and food quality. The myth that carbs cause fat gain directly through insulin has not held up under controlled metabolic research; fat gain is driven by sustained caloric surplus, regardless of macronutrient source. The rapid weight loss seen in the first week of low-carb dieting is largely water attached to glycogen stores — a physiological fact that has been widely misrepresented as proof of superior fat burning.

What Carbohydrates Actually Do In The Body

Glucose is the body's primary and preferred fuel for the brain, red blood cells, and high-intensity muscular effort. The brain alone requires approximately 120 grams of glucose daily and will trigger gluconeogenesis — the conversion of amino acids and glycerol into glucose — when dietary carbohydrates are absent. This metabolic cost is often overlooked in blanket anti-carbohydrate arguments. Muscle glycogen, synthesised from dietary carbohydrates, is the dominant fuel for any exercise above roughly 70% of maximal oxygen uptake; without it, power output and force production are compromised. Beyond energy, carbohydrates — specifically fibre — are the primary food source for the gut microbiome. Fermentation of dietary fibre produces short-chain fatty acids including butyrate, which strengthens the gut lining, regulates appetite via gut-brain signalling, and exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects throughout the body. This means that a very low-fibre diet is, paradoxically, a pro-inflammatory diet regardless of how clean the fats and proteins are.

Timing and Context Change Everything

DeLauer argues that when you eat carbohydrates is as important as what carbohydrates you eat. The post-exercise window — roughly 30 to 90 minutes following resistance training — represents a metabolically unique period where GLUT4 transporters migrate to muscle cell surfaces and glucose is taken up independently of insulin signalling. Consuming fast-absorbing carbohydrates in this window refills glycogen, amplifies muscle protein synthesis when combined with protein, and does so with minimal fat storage risk. Carb cycling — the strategic alternation between higher-carb training days and lower-carb rest days — captures the best of both metabolic worlds: improved fat oxidation and insulin sensitivity on lower-carb days, and enhanced anabolism and performance on higher-carb days. This is the model DeLauer personally follows and has advocated for as a sustainable long-term approach for body composition and metabolic health.

Quality Is the Defining Variable

Not all carbohydrates are created equal, and this is where much of the anti-carb argument goes wrong by conflating junk food with food that happens to contain carbohydrates. White bread and sweet potato share a similar macronutrient profile but are metabolically unrecognisable to the body. Whole-food carbohydrates arrive embedded in a physical matrix of fibre, water, polyphenols, and micronutrients that collectively slow digestion, blunt glucose response, nourish the microbiome, and deliver genuine nutritional value. Resistant starch — found in cooked and cooled rice, green bananas, and lentils — feeds colonic bacteria that produce butyrate and improve insulin sensitivity. Polyphenols in berries and colourful vegetables actively inhibit glucose absorption and activate AMPK, a cellular energy sensor that mimics aspects of fasting. Ultra-processed foods destroy this matrix and deliver glucose rapidly and without the biological signals that would normally accompany carbohydrate consumption in nature. The target is not low-carb; it is low-processed, high-quality, whole-food carbohydrate selection.

Metabolic Flexibility: The Real Goal

DeLauer's most significant intellectual evolution is his embrace of metabolic flexibility as the organising principle of nutritional health. A metabolically healthy person can oxidise fat efficiently when fasted or at rest, and switch seamlessly to glucose oxidation when carbohydrates are available or training intensity rises. This capacity — not the permanent elimination of carbohydrates — is what differentiates a metabolically healthy individual from someone with insulin resistance. Prolonged strict carbohydrate restriction, paradoxically, can impair glucose tolerance when carbs are eventually reintroduced, a phenomenon called physiological insulin resistance as muscles adapt fully to fat oxidation. DeLauer now explicitly frames different individuals' carbohydrate needs on a spectrum: a sedentary, metabolically compromised person may benefit significantly from carbohydrate restriction, while a lean, highly active person aggressively restricting carbs may be undermining their hormonal health, performance, and recovery without meaningful benefit. Context, not ideology, should determine carbohydrate intake.

Practical Strategies That Work

The video closes with actionable guidance. The best carbohydrate sources are sweet potato, oats, legumes, berries, and cooked-and-cooled starches for their resistant starch content. Carbohydrate intake should be timed primarily around exercise — with particular emphasis on the post-training window. Food sequencing — eating protein and fibre before carbohydrates in a meal — can reduce post-meal glucose response by up to 30% with no other dietary changes. A 10–15 minute walk after eating carbohydrates activates GLUT4 in muscle and accelerates glucose clearance, reducing the insulin burden. For individuals coming off a low-carb period, carbohydrate reintroduction should be gradual, food-quality-focused, and exercise-adjacent to allow the body to rebuild glucose tolerance smoothly. Personal glucose monitoring via CGM tools provides far more actionable insight into individual carbohydrate tolerance than population-level glycaemic index tables.

01
Stop blaming carbs for fat gain — blame caloric surplus
Controlled studies show matched fat loss on low-carb and high-carb diets when calories and protein are equal. Total energy intake, not carbohydrate grams, is the primary driver of body fat accumulation.
02
Eat carbohydrates within 30–90 minutes post-training
Post-exercise GLUT4 upregulation allows insulin-independent glucose uptake into muscle. This is the safest and most effective window to consume carbohydrates — they go to glycogen, not fat stores.
03
Eat protein and fibre before carbohydrates at each meal
Food sequencing — protein and vegetables first, carbs last — reduces post-meal glucose by up to 30%. No restriction required, just reordering what goes into your mouth first.
04
Walk for 10–15 minutes after carbohydrate-containing meals
Light post-meal movement activates GLUT4 transporters in muscle, accelerating glucose clearance from the bloodstream and reducing the insulin required to restore baseline blood sugar.
05
Cook, cool, and reheat starchy foods to increase resistant starch
Cooling cooked rice, potatoes, or lentils and eating them cold or reheated meaningfully increases resistant starch content, which feeds gut bacteria, produces butyrate, and lowers the glycaemic impact.
06
Choose whole-food carbs with intact physical structure
Sweet potato, oats, legumes, and berries contain fibre, polyphenols, and a physical food matrix that slows digestion. These are categorically different from processed carbohydrates despite similar macronutrient labels.
07
Use carb cycling if you want the best of both protocols
Eat higher-carbohydrate on training days (prioritised around workouts) and lower-carbohydrate on rest or light activity days. This improves insulin sensitivity on low days and maximises anabolism and performance on high days.
08
If coming off low-carb, reintroduce carbs gradually and around exercise
Physiological insulin resistance from prolonged carb avoidance means a sudden carbohydrate reintroduction can produce exaggerated glucose spikes. Reintroduce slowly over 1–3 weeks, exercise-adjacent, starting with root vegetables and legumes.
09
Use a CGM to personalise your carbohydrate strategy
Continuous glucose monitors reveal your individual response to specific foods — far more useful than generic glycaemic index tables. Two people can have vastly different glucose responses to identical foods based on their gut microbiome, activity history, and metabolic health.
10
Build metabolic flexibility — don't just pick a camp
The goal is not to be permanently low-carb or permanently high-carb. Metabolic flexibility — the ability to oxidise both fat and glucose efficiently depending on context — is the actual marker of metabolic health. Zone 2 cardio and resistance training are the best tools for building it alongside strategic carbohydrate intake.
