The Diary of a CEO · Dr. David Eagleman · Apr 23, 2026

Your Brain Is Not Who You Think It Is

Stanford Neuroscientist reveals the purpose of dreams, the science of habit change, and how to use AI without losing your mind.

DR. DAVID EAGLEMAN — THE BRAIN
🏛️ You Are a Neural Parliament ▼
86 billion neurons in competing networks
Different neural coalitions constantly vote on your behaviour. The "winning" coalition at any given moment determines your action — this is why you argue with yourself.
The "Team of Rivals" model of self
You are not one person. You are a parliament of sub-selves — part of you wants the cookie, part wants to diet, part wants to compromise. Self-control is a political problem, not a moral one.
Ulysses Contracts — pre-commit your future self
Remove temptation before the bad coalition gets a vote. AA clears alcohol from the house. You delete the app before deep work. Automated savings remove the decision entirely. Structure your environment, not your willpower.
"Know thyself" as a practical strategy
Review your own past behaviour honestly. Notice which contexts trigger which sub-self. That self-knowledge is the foundation of every lasting behaviour change.
🔄 Brain Plasticity — You Can Rewire Yourself ▼
Fluid vs. Crystallised Intelligence
Fluid intelligence (raw learning ability) peaks at ~age 2. Crystallised intelligence (accumulated expertise) grows with age. Adults aren't less plastic — their brains just don't need to change as often.
Physical proof: pianists, taxi drivers
London cab drivers grow a larger posterior hippocampus from memorising 25,000 streets. Pianists develop enlarged motor cortex areas for finger control. What you practise physically reshapes the brain.
Critical periods — doors that close
Language concepts must be acquired in early childhood. Romanian orphans denied social interaction developed permanent cognitive deficits. Some plasticity windows do shut — use them early.
Adults can still change — the key is challenge
The brain stops changing when it doesn't need to. Novel, difficult experiences force it to rebuild its model of the world. The pandemic — for all its horror — demonstrated that adults can massively adapt when circumstances demand it.
The "sculptor" metaphor
You are the sculptor of your own brain. Every repeated behaviour carves neural pathways. You choose which pathways to deepen through deliberate practice and challenge.
😴 Why We Dream — The Defensive Activation Theory ▼
The visual cortex is under siege every night
When your eyes close, your visual cortex receives no input. Within just 60 minutes (Harvard blindfold study), other senses — touch, hearing — begin colonising visual territory. This happens every single night.
Dreams are a biological fire drill
Ancient brainstem circuits fire random electrical signals into the visual cortex during REM sleep every ~90 minutes. This "defensive activation" repels the sensory takeover and preserves your visual system.
Dream narratives are a side effect
The brain is a compulsive pattern-matcher. It takes the random defensive signals and weaves them into stories — which we call dreams. The story is not the point; the activation is.
REM sleep ∝ brain plasticity across species
Human infants spend ~50% of sleep in REM (high plasticity). Adults ~20%. Animals with more plastic brains get more REM. Dreaming is not a quirk — it is essential maintenance.
Not remembering dreams ≠ not dreaming
Dream memory is a separate process from having the dream. You are almost certainly defending your visual cortex every night whether you recall it or not. However, consistently no REM sleep is a warning sign.
🤖 AI — Amplifier or Atrophy Machine? ▼
Virtuous vs. Vicious Friction
Vicious friction = tedious repetitive tasks (data entry, formatting). Automate these. Virtuous friction = hard, novel thinking challenges. Do NOT outsource these — they are what keeps your brain plastic. The danger of AI is eliminating both indiscriminately.
Use AI as a brutally honest critic
Don't ask AI to validate you. Ask it to find flaws in your plan, play devil's advocate, identify your blind spots. The pressure-free private dialogue sharpens critical thinking faster than most human conversations.
AI is a "motorcycle for the mind"
Like the motorcycle amplifies the human body's travel ability, AI amplifies cognitive capacity. The direction of amplification depends entirely on how you use it. It can make you sharper or lazier — you decide.
AI remixes — humans select
AI is excellent at generating novel combinations from existing data. It lacks human discernment: knowing which creation will resonate, matter, or endure. That curatorial faculty — taste — remains distinctly human.
Social media's effect: narrowing, polarising
Engagement algorithms feed you content confirming existing beliefs, narrowing your neural model of the world rather than expanding it. Actively seeking content that challenges your worldview is now a deliberate, counter-cultural act.
🧩 Cognitive Reserve & Ageing Well ▼
The Catholic Nuns Study
Nuns who remained intellectually and socially active stayed cognitively sharp into old age — even when their post-mortem brains showed full Alzheimer's pathology. Their mental challenge had built resilience that masked the disease.
Retirement can accelerate cognitive decline
Early retirement cuts off the messy, effortful social interactions that drive plasticity. Studies suggest early retirement is correlated with earlier death and faster cognitive decline. Stay engaged.
"Nothing is as hard for the brain as other people"
Human social interaction demands empathy, negotiation, emotional reading, and real-time adaptation — making it one of the most powerful drivers of brain plasticity. Isolation is cognitively damaging.
The sweet spot: between frustration and achievement
The optimal zone for brain growth is tasks that are hard but achievable — the edge of your ability. Too easy = no growth. Too hard = shutdown. Seek that edge deliberately and consistently.
🌍 The Next 10 Years & Human Connection ▼
Real-world experiences making a comeback
After a decade of digital-first living, people are seeking tactile, embodied, offline experiences — live events, crafts, physical community. This is a neurological craving for the full-sensory engagement screens can't provide.
Human brains are radically individual
Aphantasia (no visual imagery) vs. Hyperfantasia (ultra-vivid imagery) — most people don't know where they sit on this spectrum. Brain differences are vast and often invisible. There is no single "right" way for a brain to work.
The social media company of 2026
Eagleman predicts a market opportunity for a platform designed not for engagement at any cost, but to connect people on shared interests — then slowly introduce differing opinions to reduce polarisation. Algorithm design as a public health tool.
Human connection more critical than ever
In an AI-saturated world, the irreplaceable value is genuine human relationship — unpredictable, effortful, and neurologically nourishing in ways no algorithm can replicate. Protect and prioritise it.

Overview

Stanford neuroscientist Dr. David Eagleman joins Steven Bartlett to challenge almost every assumption we hold about how our minds work. Across 93 minutes, Eagleman builds one coherent argument: the brain is not a fixed, unified "you" — it is a living, competitive, constantly rewiring system. Everything else in the episode flows from that premise.

1 — You Are a Neural Parliament

Eagleman opens by dismantling the illusion of a single self. Your 86 billion neurons form competing networks — coalitions with different drives, time horizons, and values. When you "argue with yourself" about eating the cookie or skipping the gym, that is a genuine political contest between neural sub-selves. The winner at any moment determines your action.

The practical implication is the Ulysses Contract: bind your future self now, before the wrong coalition is in power. Clear the alcohol from the house, delete the app, set up the automatic savings transfer. These pre-commitments bypass the internal vote entirely by changing the environment rather than fighting willpower.

2 — Brain Plasticity: You Really Can Change

The brain is "livewired" — it physically restructures itself in response to what you do. London cab drivers grow larger hippocampi. Pianists grow enlarged motor cortexes. Blind people repurpose their visual cortex for touch. This is not metaphor; it is measurable anatomy.

Fluid intelligence (learn-anything ability) peaks around age 2, then slowly shifts to crystallised intelligence (expertise and accumulated knowledge). But plasticity never stops — it just activates less because the brain doesn't need to change. Novel challenge is the key that unlocks it again in adulthood. The pandemic, for all its damage, was a forced plasticity event: everyone had to remodel their mental map of the world.

3 — Why We Dream (The Defensive Activation Theory)

This is the episode's most striking insight. Every night, when your eyes close, your visual cortex goes dark. Within 60 minutes — proven in a Harvard blindfold experiment — other senses begin colonising that unused neural territory. Without intervention, hearing and touch would gradually take over the visual cortex, degrading sight.

The brain's solution, evolved over millions of years, is dreaming. Ancient brainstem circuits fire random electrical bursts into the visual cortex every ~90 minutes during REM sleep — a biological fire drill that repels sensory invasion and preserves visual territory. The narrative we experience as a dream is simply the brain's pattern-matching machinery making sense of those random signals. The dream story is a side effect; the defensive activation is the point.

More plastic brains get more REM. Human infants spend ~50% of sleep in REM; adults ~20%. Not remembering your dreams doesn't mean you're not having them — but consistently poor REM sleep is a genuine structural warning.

4 — AI: Cognitive Amplifier or Atrophy Machine?

Eagleman is genuinely optimistic about AI — he calls it a "motorcycle for the mind." But the direction of amplification depends entirely on how you use it. He draws the crucial distinction between vicious friction (tedious, repetitive tasks — automate these without guilt) and virtuous friction (hard, novel cognitive challenges — protect these fiercely).

The danger is treating AI as a way to avoid all friction. Outsourcing your thinking also outsources your brain's exercise. The optimal approach: use AI as a brutally honest critic. Ask it to attack your plan, find your blind spots, steelman the counter-argument. That form of engagement deepens thinking rather than replacing it.

On creativity: AI excels at generating novel recombinations of existing data but lacks human taste — the curatorial faculty that knows which creation will matter. That selection ability remains distinctly human for now.

5 — Cognitive Reserve, Ageing, and the Power of Social Challenge

The famous Catholic nuns study found that women who remained intellectually and socially active stayed cognitively sharp into very old age — even when autopsy revealed their brains had full Alzheimer's pathology. They had built such deep cognitive reserve that the disease had nothing to grip.

The single most demanding cognitive activity, Eagleman argues, is other people. Human social interaction — with its real-time demands on empathy, negotiation, and emotional reading — is one of the most powerful plasticity drivers available. Early retirement severs exactly those interactions, which may explain why it correlates with faster cognitive decline and earlier mortality. Stay socially and mentally engaged as long as possible.

6 — The Future: Connection as the Scarcest Resource

Eagleman closes with a forward-looking view. As AI handles more cognitive tasks and screens mediate more interaction, the neurologically irreplaceable resource becomes genuine human connection — unpredictable, effortful, and deeply nourishing. He predicts a strong cultural counter-movement toward tactile, embodied, offline experience and sees a market opportunity for social platforms designed not to maximise engagement at any cost but to reduce polarisation and build real relationships.

01

Build Ulysses Contracts, Not Willpower

Before the "wrong" version of you is in charge, remove the temptation. Clear junk food, delete the app, set the automatic transfer. Pre-commitment is neurologically superior to in-the-moment resistance.

02

Audit Your Self-Knowledge Honestly

Review your past behaviour across contexts. Notice which situations trigger which sub-self. The ancient instruction "know thyself" is a practical neuroscience tool, not a philosophical platitude.

03

Seek the Frustration–Achievement Edge

The optimal zone for brain rewiring is tasks that are hard but achievable. Deliberately put yourself at your ability limit — in skills, sports, conversations, creative work — and stay there regularly.

04

Protect Your REM Sleep

Sleep is not rest — it is structural maintenance for your visual system and overall brain health. Consistently poor REM sleep allows sensory takeover of your visual cortex. Treat sleep quality as non-negotiable.

05

Use AI to Attack Your Ideas, Not Confirm Them

Prompt AI to find flaws, steelman opponents, and identify assumptions you've missed. This adversarial use sharpens thinking. Using AI to validate what you already believe is cognitively worthless.

06

Distinguish Virtuous from Vicious Friction

Automate tedious repetitive tasks (vicious friction). Protect hard, novel thinking challenges (virtuous friction). If AI is eliminating the challenges that make you think, it is shrinking your brain, not expanding it.

07

Never Fully Retire Your Mind

Social engagement and intellectual challenge are the best-evidenced interventions against cognitive decline. Retirement from work does not have to mean retirement from demanding mental and social activity. Build cognitive reserve early.

08

Treat Other People as Brain Training

Human social interaction is among the most cognitively demanding activities available. Unpredictable, emotionally complex relationships are neurologically nourishing in ways no algorithm can replicate. Prioritise face-to-face connection.

09

Actively Counteract Your Algorithm's Narrowing Effect

Social media feeds are engineered to narrow your mental model. Deliberately seek content, people, and experiences that contradict your existing worldview to keep your brain's model of reality wide and plastic.

10

Pursue Novelty as a Health Strategy

New experiences — new routes, new skills, new languages, new social contexts — force the brain to rebuild its models. Novelty is not entertainment; it is one of the most evidence-backed tools for maintaining cognitive flexibility across a lifetime.
