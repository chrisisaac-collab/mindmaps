Cancer Is a Metabolic Disease, Not a Genetic One
▼
Otto Warburg's 1924 discovery — the ignored foundation ▶
Nobel laureate Otto Warburg first observed in the 1920s that cancer cells preferentially ferment glucose into lactate even in the presence of adequate oxygen — a phenomenon now called the Warburg Effect or aerobic glycolysis. Normal cells use oxygen to produce ATP efficiently through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Cancer cells appear to abandon this efficient pathway and revert to a far less efficient fermentation process that produces far less ATP per glucose molecule. Warburg proposed this was caused by damaged respiratory function — but mainstream oncology moved away from his metabolic framework in favour of the somatic mutation theory after Watson and Crick's DNA discovery.
The mainstream theory vs. Seyfried's challenge ▶
The dominant paradigm in oncology — the somatic mutation theory — holds that cancer is caused by accumulating genetic mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, and that these mutations are the primary driver of uncontrolled cell proliferation. Seyfried argues this is backwards: the genetic mutations observed in cancer cells are downstream consequences of the primary event, which is mitochondrial damage and dysfunction. Mutated, dysfunctional mitochondria lose control of the cell nucleus, causing the cell to revert to an ancient, primitive fermentation phenotype. The mutations are symptoms, not causes.
The Oncogenic Paradox — Szent-Györgyi's challenge ▶
Albert von Szent-Györgyi (another Nobel laureate) articulated what Seyfried calls the "Oncogenic Paradox": if cancer is caused by genetic mutations, how do we explain the fact that you can take the nucleus of a cancer cell, transplant it into a healthy cell with normal cytoplasm (and therefore normal mitochondria), and the cancer phenotype disappears? Conversely, transplanting healthy nuclei into cancer cell cytoplasm (with damaged mitochondria) can produce cancer-like behaviour. These nuclear transfer experiments, Seyfried argues, definitively demonstrate that cancer is a cytoplasmic disease — driven by mitochondrial dysfunction — not a nuclear/genetic one.
Ghost mitochondria — what cancer cells look like under the microscope ▶
Under electron microscopy, cancer cells contain what Seyfried calls "ghost mitochondria" — organelles that have lost their normal internal structure (cristae) and appear as empty, ghost-like shells. These structurally damaged mitochondria cannot conduct efficient oxidative phosphorylation. They are present but non-functional. This structural evidence corroborates the metabolic data showing that cancer cells cannot generate ATP through normal respiratory pathways and must therefore rely on fermentation. The mitochondrial damage is observable at the cellular level — this is not theoretical.
Why this matters — 100 years of the wrong target ▶
Seyfried's central argument is that mainstream oncology has spent a century targeting the wrong thing: treating genetic mutations as the primary cause when they are consequences of the underlying metabolic problem. This explains, he argues, why the "war on cancer" has been so disappointing despite enormous investment — you can mutate the mutations all you like, but the mitochondria remain damaged and the fermentation phenotype persists. The metabolic theory opens a completely different therapeutic window: instead of trying to mutate or poison cancer cells, starve them by removing the specific fuels they depend on.

The Two Fuels: Glucose & Glutamine
▼
Why cancer cells can't use fat or ketones ▶
Normal cells can switch between fuels — glucose, fatty acids, and ketone bodies — with great flexibility, depending on availability and metabolic state. Cancer cells have lost this flexibility because their mitochondria are damaged. To oxidise fatty acids or ketones, you need functional mitochondria and an intact electron transport chain. Cancer cells lack this capacity. They are metabolically locked into fermentation pathways. This is a critical vulnerability — and it's the biological basis for the therapeutic hypothesis that a ketogenic diet, by shifting the body from glucose metabolism to ketone/fatty acid metabolism, creates a metabolic environment that starves tumour cells while leaving healthy cells unharmed.
Glucose — the first cancer fuel (cytoplasmic fermentation) ▶
Glucose fermentation in cancer cells occurs in the cytoplasm via the glycolytic pathway, producing ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation (SLP) rather than through the mitochondrial electron transport chain. This process generates lactic acid as a byproduct — the Warburg Effect. It is highly inefficient: 2 ATP per glucose molecule versus ~36 ATP from full oxidative phosphorylation. But cancer cells compensate by dramatically upregulating glucose uptake (via GLUT transporter overexpression) and running the glycolytic pathway at high speed. This is why PET scans, which detect high glucose uptake, can identify and locate tumours throughout the body.
Glutamine — the second cancer fuel (mitochondrial fermentation) ▶
Seyfried's key update to Warburg's original theory: cancer cells also ferment the amino acid glutamine inside their damaged mitochondria — a process called mitochondrial substrate-level phosphorylation. Glutamine enters the TCA cycle and, through a modified, truncated version of normal mitochondrial metabolism, generates additional ATP and crucially provides biosynthetic building blocks (amino acids, nucleotides, lipids) that rapidly dividing cells need. This discovery explains why some cancers can survive glucose restriction alone — they switch to or increase glutamine usage. Targeting both fuels simultaneously is therefore essential to the press-pulse strategy.
Why children get cancer — the glutamine connection ▶
One of the most counterintuitive aspects of Seyfried's framework is that it explains pediatric cancer. If cancer is a metabolic disease driven by mitochondrial damage from environmental toxins, poor diet, and chronic inflammation — factors associated with adult lifestyle — why do babies and young children develop cancer? Seyfried argues that early-stage foetal and neonatal development is characterised by extremely high glutamine availability and utilisation (glutamine drives rapid cell division and growth). Under certain conditions of prenatal carcinogen exposure or genetic susceptibility that compromises mitochondrial function, this developmental glutamine abundance can fuel cancer cell growth in otherwise healthy-seeming individuals.
How cancer spreads — metastasis as a metabolic migration ▶
The conventional explanation for metastasis — that cells develop new genetic mutations enabling migration — doesn't fully satisfy Seyfried. He proposes that metastatic cells have undergone a macrophage-like transformation: they have fused with or adopted the characteristics of immune cells (which are naturally migratory) through a process of mitochondrial exchange. This hybrid cell type can survive in the blood, migrate to new sites, and re-establish tumours. Critically, these metastatic cells are still dependent on glucose and glutamine for fermentation — making them, in principle, as susceptible to metabolic therapy as primary tumours.

The Glucose Ketone Index (GKI)
▼
What it is and how to calculate it ▶
The Glucose Ketone Index was co-developed by Seyfried's lab as a single-number biomarker reflecting the metabolic state of the body. Formula: blood glucose (in mmol/L) divided by blood ketones (in mmol/L). Both measurements require a finger-prick blood test — a glucometer for glucose and a ketone meter (such as a Keto-Mojo or Abbott Precision Xtra) for beta-hydroxybutyrate. A GKI of 1.0 means blood glucose and blood ketones are at equal concentrations — indicating deep therapeutic ketosis. Seyfried measured his own GKI live on the podcast to demonstrate how accessible self-monitoring is.
The GKI zones — from metabolic chaos to therapeutic range ▶
GKI above 9: standard mixed-fuel metabolism — essentially no ketosis, high glucose relative to ketones, typical of most people on a standard diet. GKI 6–9: very light metabolic shift, achievable through moderate low-carb eating. GKI 3–6: moderate therapeutic ketosis — the band most people aim for with a general ketogenic diet, associated with meaningful metabolic health benefits. GKI 1–3: deep therapeutic ketosis — the target range for oncology and neurology protocols. GKI below 1: extreme ketosis, typically only achieved under extended fasting or strict medically supervised protocols. The transition from metabolic chaos (GKI >9) to the therapeutic zone (GKI <3) is the practical goal of dietary intervention.
The "red zone" — what a high GKI means for cancer risk ▶
At a GKI above 9, the body is running predominantly on glucose with negligible ketones. Seyfried frames this — the metabolic state of most people eating a standard Western diet — as the "red zone" for cancer risk. High circulating blood glucose provides an abundant supply of the primary cancer fuel; elevated insulin creates anabolic growth signals that support tumour development; and low ketones provide no metabolic competition for cancer cells' glucose dependency. Chronic habitation in the high-GKI zone, according to Seyfried, is a significant contributor to the chronic disease and cancer epidemic in industrialised nations.
How to lower your GKI — the dietary levers ▶
Moving from a high to a low GKI requires two simultaneous changes: reducing blood glucose (by restricting carbohydrates) and increasing blood ketones (by increasing fat metabolism through either dietary fat or fasting). The most effective dietary approach is a very-low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet: typically under 20 grams of net carbohydrates per day, with moderate protein and high fat. Fasting accelerates the transition — even a 16-hour overnight fast will meaningfully lower GKI in most people. Physical exercise also lowers blood glucose and increases ketone production. The combination of fasting + ketogenic diet is the most powerful non-pharmacological way to reach a therapeutic GKI.
GKI for prevention vs. therapeutic use ▶
For prevention in healthy individuals, Seyfried suggests aiming for a GKI below 3 regularly — achievable through a well-managed low-carbohydrate diet without requiring extreme measures. For active therapeutic use in cancer management, the target is GKI below 1, which typically requires a strict ketogenic diet combined with intermittent fasting and often extended water fasting periods. Maintaining a GKI below 1 continuously is demanding and should be done with medical supervision, particularly for cancer patients undergoing conventional treatment simultaneously. For prevention, even periodic GKI reductions (achieved through regular fasting or low-carb periods) are likely beneficial relative to chronic high-GKI living.

The Press-Pulse Protocol
▼
The "press" — sustained metabolic stress through ketogenic diet ▶
The "press" component is a continuous, sustained metabolic intervention designed to chronically reduce tumour fuel availability. This means maintaining a strict ketogenic diet (very low carbohydrate, moderate protein, high fat) combined with structured fasting to keep the GKI below 1. This sustained low-glucose, high-ketone metabolic state creates chronic metabolic stress on tumour cells — they are deprived of their primary fuel source on an ongoing basis. Healthy cells adapt readily to this metabolic shift and fuel themselves on ketones and fatty acids. Cancer cells cannot. The press alone is not sufficient to eliminate most established tumours, but it creates the metabolic foundation that makes the "pulse" interventions far more effective.
The "pulse" — intermittent targeted stressors ▶
Layered on top of the press, pulse interventions are intermittent, targeted stressors designed to strike the metabolically weakened cancer cells. These include: hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) — exploiting cancer cells' inability to detoxify reactive oxygen species (ROS); glutamine inhibitors (such as DON or its prodrug JHU-083) — targeting the second cancer fuel simultaneously with the press's glucose restriction; intravenous vitamin C at pharmacological doses — which generates ROS preferentially inside cancer cells; and conventional chemotherapy or radiation at significantly reduced doses. The press-pulse combination in preclinical models has shown dramatic synergistic effects exceeding what either approach achieves independently.
Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) — the ROS pulse ▶
HBOT involves breathing 100% oxygen at 2–3 times atmospheric pressure, dramatically elevating blood and tissue oxygen levels. In normal healthy cells, elevated oxygen generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) — free radicals — but the cells detoxify these efficiently through antioxidant enzyme systems including SOD and catalase. Cancer cells, with their damaged mitochondria and compromised antioxidant capacity, cannot handle this oxidative challenge — the elevated ROS overwhelms their defences and triggers cell death. HBOT, used as a pulse on top of the ketogenic diet press, has shown meaningful anti-tumour effects in preclinical models and in case reports of human patients including Pablo Kelly.
Pablo Kelly — 10 years surviving "terminal" glioblastoma ▶
Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive primary brain tumour, with a median survival of 12–15 months with standard-of-care treatment (surgery, radiation, temozolomide chemotherapy). Pablo Kelly, from Devon, England, was diagnosed with GBM at age 26, declined conventional treatment, and instead adopted a strict metabolic therapy protocol based on Seyfried's work — ketogenic diet, fasting, HBOT, and GKI monitoring. He is documented as surviving more than 10 years post-diagnosis. Seyfried features his case as compelling evidence that the metabolic approach can provide extraordinary outcomes even in what is conventionally considered an untreatable, terminal cancer.
Why this option isn't offered — the systemic problem ▶
Seyfried acknowledges directly that the metabolic approach is rarely if ever presented to cancer patients as an option, even in a palliative context. He identifies several reasons: the oncology system is structured around FDA-approved pharmacological and radiation protocols, outside of which clinicians face significant professional risk; there is no commercial incentive to fund large trials of non-patentable dietary interventions; and the genetic theory framework, which dominates academic oncology education, leaves no conceptual space for metabolic approaches. He argues this represents a systemic failure that costs lives — and that oncology researchers who work in this field face active professional marginalisation. The confidential envelope section of the episode reinforced his frustration with institutional suppression of this research.

What Damages Mitochondria & Feeds Cancer
▼
Chronic stress — cortisol is a cancer fuel delivery system ▶
Psychological stress triggers the release of cortisol and adrenaline (catecholamines), both of which raise blood glucose — cortisol by stimulating gluconeogenesis in the liver, adrenaline by mobilising glycogen stores. For cancer patients, this creates a direct physiological link between mental state and tumour fuel supply: sustained anxiety, fear, and depression elevate blood glucose, raise GKI, and provide more fuel to cancer cells. This is why Seyfried considers stress management — not just a wellbeing issue — a metabolic therapeutic variable that should be integrated into cancer treatment protocols. A patient who is calm and in ketosis is metabolically very different from a patient who is anxious and hyperglycaemic.
Poor sleep — the overnight metabolic disruption ▶
Sleep deprivation elevates cortisol and impairs insulin sensitivity, causing blood glucose to rise and stay elevated — directly worsening GKI and creating a more favourable metabolic environment for cancer growth. Beyond the glucose effect, chronic sleep disruption compromises mitochondrial function: sleep is the primary period during which cells conduct mitochondrial quality-control processes including mitophagy (the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria). Seyfried frames poor sleep not as a performance or wellness issue but as a direct mitochondrial health risk — and therefore a cancer risk. Sleep optimisation belongs in any serious cancer prevention protocol.
Microplastics and forever chemicals (PFAS) — the new carcinogens ▶
PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances), sometimes called "forever chemicals," and microplastics are ubiquitous environmental contaminants that have been shown to accumulate in human tissue, including the liver, kidneys, and increasingly the brain. Both classes of compounds have been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction through multiple mechanisms: disrupting mitochondrial membrane integrity, impairing electron transport chain function, and triggering inflammatory responses that damage mitochondria. As these contaminants have become increasingly prevalent in food packaging, cookware, water supplies, and air, Seyfried argues they represent a significant contributing factor to rising cancer rates — particularly in affluent, industrialised nations with high plastic usage.
Why wealthy countries have more cancer ▶
The correlation between national wealth and cancer incidence — which runs opposite to the pattern for most infectious diseases — is explained by Seyfried through the metabolic lens. Wealthy nations have higher consumption of ultra-processed food (driving chronic hyperglycaemia), higher obesity rates (which independently impair mitochondrial function and elevate cancer risk via insulin/IGF-1 signalling), greater exposure to synthetic chemicals and plastics, higher rates of psychological stress associated with competitive economies, and modern sleep disruption from artificial light and digital technology. Cancer, in this framing, is a disease of metabolic affluence — the cumulative result of an environment that systematically damages mitochondria over decades.
Other mitochondria-damaging carcinogens ▶
Beyond the metabolic diet-and-lifestyle factors, Seyfried discusses classical carcinogens through his mitochondrial lens: ionising radiation damages mitochondrial DNA and structure; chemical carcinogens (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from grilled meat and cigarette smoke, asbestos, benzene) induce mitochondrial dysfunction directly; chronic inflammation — from gut dysbiosis, autoimmune conditions, or persistent infection — generates oxidative and nitrosative stress that damages mitochondria over time. Prenatal carcinogen exposure is also highlighted as an underappreciated pathway to childhood cancer: maternal exposure to carcinogens during pregnancy can damage foetal mitochondria before birth, establishing a metabolic vulnerability that may manifest as cancer years or decades later.

The Prevention & Action Plan
▼
What to eat — foods that support metabolic health ▶
Seyfried's dietary recommendations prioritise foods that reduce blood glucose, support ketone production, and minimise inflammatory burden. Animal proteins and fats are central: fatty fish (wild salmon, sardines, mackerel), grass-fed meat, eggs, organ meats. Non-starchy vegetables: leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflower, courgette, cucumber. Healthy fats: olive oil, avocados, coconut oil, grass-fed butter. The dietary goal is to eliminate virtually all refined carbohydrates and added sugars, minimise starchy vegetables and legumes, and achieve a macronutrient ratio of roughly 70-75% fat, 20-25% protein, 5% carbohydrates — sufficient to consistently maintain a low GKI. Quality matters: organic and grass-fed sources reduce exposure to pesticides and synthetic hormones that may compound mitochondrial damage.
What to avoid — the foods that feed cancer ▶
All sugar and refined carbohydrates directly raise blood glucose and insulin, elevating GKI. This includes: sugar, honey, fruit juice, soft drinks, bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, breakfast cereals, most fruits, and any ultra-processed food containing sugar or starch. Seed oils (rapeseed, sunflower, soybean, corn oil) are implicated in mitochondrial membrane disruption through their high omega-6 content and oxidation during processing. Processed meats with nitrates/nitrites. Alcohol — metabolised to acetaldehyde, which directly damages mitochondrial DNA. "Hitting the wall" in fasting (the uncomfortable transition phase) is also discussed — Seyfried acknowledges it's real but temporary, and the metabolic benefits on the other side are significant.
Fasting protocols — how to use them for prevention ▶
Seyfried discusses fasting as both a metabolic tool and a way to initiate and deepen therapeutic ketosis. For prevention, a daily 16-18 hour intermittent fast (eating within a 6-8 hour window) is sufficient to meaningfully lower GKI and initiate ketone production in most people. Periodic extended fasting — 3 to 5 days of water fasting — can achieve a GKI below 1 and provide deep metabolic housecleaning, including aggressive mitophagy (clearance of damaged mitochondria). For cancer patients undergoing treatment, more structured fasting protocols — timed to coincide with chemotherapy cycles — have been shown in clinical research to reduce side effects and improve treatment efficacy by making cancer cells more susceptible and healthy cells more resilient.
The #1 actionable takeaway from Seyfried ▶
When Bartlett asks Seyfried for his single most important actionable recommendation, his answer is to measure and manage your Glucose Ketone Index. Know where you are metabolically — right now. Buy a glucometer and a ketone meter (combined devices are available), test in the morning after an overnight fast, calculate your GKI (glucose mmol/L ÷ ketones mmol/L), and use that number to guide dietary decisions. Most people will find their fasted GKI is above 9 — standard metabolic chaos. The goal is to begin the transition toward a GKI below 3 through dietary changes, and to understand the connection between what you eat, your metabolic state, and your long-term cancer risk.
Resources — Seyfried's book, foundation, and research ▶
Seyfried's foundational text is "Cancer as a Metabolic Disease: On the Origin, Management, and Prevention of Cancer" — a comprehensive scientific treatment of the full theory, evidence, and therapeutic framework. 🔬 The Core Argument: Cancer Is Not What Oncology Thinks It Is

Professor Thomas Seyfried has spent decades at Boston College building the scientific case for a radical reframing of cancer. His central argument: cancer is fundamentally a disease of mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic dysregulation — not a genetic disease, as mainstream oncology has maintained for the past 70 years.

The conventional somatic mutation theory holds that cancer arises from accumulating DNA mutations in proto-oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, which cause cells to lose normal growth regulation. Seyfried doesn't deny that mutations exist in cancer cells — he argues they are downstream consequences of the primary event, not its cause. The primary event is mitochondrial failure.

"If cancer were truly a genetic disease, then why, when you take the nucleus of a cancer cell and put it into a healthy cell with normal mitochondria, does the cancer phenotype disappear? The genome didn't change — the mitochondria did."

The evidence for this comes from nuclear transfer experiments going back to the work of Nobel laureate Albert von Szent-Györgyi. When cancerous nuclei are transplanted into healthy cytoplasm (healthy mitochondria), the cancer phenotype largely disappears. When healthy nuclei are transplanted into cancerous cytoplasm (damaged mitochondria), normal cells begin exhibiting cancer-like behaviour. This suggests it is the cytoplasm — specifically the mitochondria — that drives the cancer phenotype, not the nuclear genome.

Under electron microscopy, cancer cells show "ghost mitochondria" — structurally empty organelles that have lost their normal cristae and cannot conduct efficient oxidative phosphorylation. Stripped of the ability to generate energy through normal aerobic respiration, these cells revert to an ancient, primitive fermentation pathway that all cells retain from early evolutionary history.

⛽ Glucose and Glutamine: The Two Fuels Cancer Can't Live Without

Seyfried's framework identifies two and only two metabolic pathways that cancer cells can exploit for energy: glucose fermentation (the Warburg Effect, operating in the cytoplasm) and glutamine fermentation (operating in the damaged mitochondria). Healthy cells can use fatty acids and ketone bodies — cancer cells cannot, because these fuels require functional mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation that cancer cells have lost.

Glucose is fermented in the cytoplasm via glycolysis, producing ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation and generating lactic acid as a byproduct. This is the process Otto Warburg first described in the 1920s — and why PET scans (which use radiolabelled glucose uptake as a tumour marker) work. Seyfried updated Warburg's framework to include a second fuel pathway: the fermentation of glutamine inside the damaged mitochondria, providing additional ATP and the biosynthetic building blocks — amino acids, nucleotides, lipids — needed for rapid cell division. This dual-fuel dependency is the reason some cancers survive glucose restriction alone: they upregulate glutamine fermentation to compensate.

The therapeutic implication is direct: if you remove both fuels simultaneously, cancer cells have no metabolic backup. Healthy cells, which retain full mitochondrial flexibility, can thrive on ketones and fatty acids in the meantime. This asymmetric metabolic vulnerability is, Seyfried argues, the most significant and underexploited feature of cancer biology.

📊 The Glucose Ketone Index: Measuring Your Metabolic State

The GKI was developed by Seyfried's lab as a quantitative biomarker to track the efficacy of metabolic therapy. Calculated as blood glucose (mmol/L) divided by blood ketones (mmol/L), it collapses two measurements into a single number that reflects how deeply the body has shifted away from glucose metabolism. A GKI of 9 or above represents standard metabolic chaos — high glucose, negligible ketones, the metabolic state of most people on a Western diet. A GKI of 1 represents equipoise between glucose and ketones — deep therapeutic ketosis.

Seyfried demonstrated measuring his own GKI live during the episode, using a finger-prick glucometer and a ketone meter. The simplicity of self-monitoring is a deliberate part of the message: this is not a specialised laboratory test requiring medical access. Anyone can track their GKI at home, understand where they sit metabolically, and use that data to guide dietary choices.

For cancer prevention in otherwise healthy people, Seyfried recommends regularly achieving a GKI below 3 — achievable through a well-managed ketogenic diet with periodic fasting. For active cancer therapy, the target is GKI below 1, which creates sufficient metabolic stress on tumour cells to make them meaningfully vulnerable to pulse interventions. Chronic habitation in the high-GKI "red zone" — which characterises the vast majority of people in industrialised nations — is, in Seyfried's framework, a significant and modifiable cancer risk factor.

💊 The Press-Pulse Protocol: A New Framework for Cancer Treatment

The press-pulse strategy is Seyfried's proposed therapeutic framework, designed to exploit cancer cells' metabolic vulnerabilities systematically. The "press" is a sustained, continuous intervention — typically a strict ketogenic diet combined with intermittent fasting — that chronically restricts glucose availability and maintains a low GKI. This creates ongoing metabolic stress on tumour cells by cutting off their primary fuel. The "pulse" consists of intermittent, targeted stressors layered on top of the press: hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), glutamine inhibitors, intravenous vitamin C, or conventional chemotherapy at reduced doses.

HBOT works by a specific mechanism: cancer cells, with their damaged antioxidant systems, cannot efficiently neutralise the surge of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by breathing high-concentration oxygen at elevated pressure. Normal healthy cells, with intact antioxidant enzyme systems, handle this oxidative challenge without damage. HBOT therefore creates selective oxidative stress that preferentially kills cancer cells — it is a metabolic pulse that amplifies the damage already being inflicted by the ketogenic press.

The case of Pablo Kelly is Seyfried's most dramatic clinical illustration. Diagnosed with glioblastoma multiforme at 26 — a brain tumour that kills most patients within 15 months even with aggressive standard treatment — Kelly declined conventional therapy and adopted a strict metabolic protocol. He has survived more than 10 years. Seyfried uses this case not as proof but as a signal: outcomes like Kelly's are simply not achievable within the standard-of-care framework, and the fact that almost no cancer patient is ever told this option exists represents, in Seyfried's view, a systemic ethical failure.

🌍 What Damages Your Mitochondria Every Day

Seyfried identifies several drivers of mitochondrial damage that are endemic to modern life. Chronic psychological stress is a direct metabolic risk: cortisol and adrenaline raise blood glucose, elevate GKI, and sustain a metabolic environment favourable to cancer cell growth. He argues that stress management is not a soft wellness add-on to cancer care — it is a metabolic intervention with direct biochemical consequences. A patient chronically anxious about their diagnosis is metabolically undermining their ketogenic diet through their own hormonal response.

Poor sleep compounds this: sleep deprivation impairs insulin sensitivity, elevates cortisol, and — critically — disrupts the mitochondrial quality-control processes (particularly mitophagy) that clear damaged mitochondria. It is during sleep that cells perform the maintenance that prevents mitochondrial dysfunction from accumulating. Microplastics and PFAS ("forever chemicals") represent a newer and more pervasive threat: these compounds accumulate in human tissues and have been shown to impair mitochondrial membrane integrity and electron transport chain function. Their ubiquity in food packaging, cookware, clothing, and water supplies makes avoidance difficult but partial reduction meaningful.

The epidemiological pattern — more cancer in wealthier, more industrialised nations — reflects this convergence of metabolic insults: processed food environments, sedentary lifestyles, chemical exposures, stress cultures, and artificially disrupted sleep. Cancer, in Seyfried's framework, is as much a disease of modern civilisation as it is of individual biology.

10 key insights from Prof. Thomas Seyfried's research

1

Cancer is fundamentally a metabolic disease — the genetic mutations are downstream effects

Nuclear transfer experiments show that cancerous nuclei in healthy mitochondria stop behaving like cancer, and healthy nuclei in cancerous mitochondria begin behaving like cancer. The primary event is mitochondrial failure, not genetic mutation. This reframing opens an entirely different therapeutic window.

2

Cancer cells can only use two fuels — glucose and glutamine — and cannot use fat or ketones

Damaged mitochondria strip cancer cells of metabolic flexibility. They cannot oxidise fatty acids or ketone bodies. This is a specific, targetable vulnerability: shift the body into a state where glucose is low and ketones are high, and you deprive tumour cells of fuel while healthy cells thrive on the alternative energy source.

3

Measure your Glucose Ketone Index — it's the most actionable metabolic number you don't know

GKI = blood glucose (mmol/L) ÷ blood ketones (mmol/L). A fasted morning GKI above 9 means you're in metabolic "red zone." Below 3 is the metabolic health target. Below 1 is the therapeutic cancer zone. Both measurements require only a finger-prick test available at any pharmacy.

4

The press-pulse protocol: sustained ketogenic diet + intermittent targeted stressors

The press creates chronic metabolic stress on tumour cells by restricting glucose (via ketogenic diet + fasting). The pulse — hyperbaric oxygen, glutamine inhibitors, IV vitamin C, or reduced-dose chemo — strikes the already-weakened cells. The combination is far more effective than either approach alone. Standard-of-care chemo with a keto press is not the same as chemo alone.

5

Pablo Kelly survived glioblastoma for 10+ years on metabolic therapy — this option exists but is almost never offered

GBM kills most patients within 15 months even with aggressive standard treatment. Kelly declined conventional therapy, adopted Seyfried's metabolic protocol, and survived over a decade. This outcome is not replicable within the standard-of-care framework, and almost no patient with this diagnosis is ever told this option exists.

6

Chronic stress directly feeds cancer — cortisol raises blood glucose, elevating tumour fuel supply

Psychological stress is not just a wellbeing issue for cancer patients — it is a metabolic variable. Cortisol and adrenaline raise blood glucose, elevate GKI, and provide more fuel to tumour cells. Stress management is a metabolic intervention, not a soft add-on. A patient who is calm and in deep ketosis is in a fundamentally different metabolic state from one who is anxious and hyperglycaemic.

7

Poor sleep is a mitochondrial health crisis — it disrupts the quality-control that prevents cancer

Sleep is when cells perform mitophagy — the selective clearance of damaged mitochondria. Chronic poor sleep allows damaged mitochondria to accumulate, the key upstream event in Seyfried's cancer model. It also raises cortisol and impairs insulin sensitivity, worsening GKI. Optimising sleep is a direct cancer-prevention strategy.

8

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is a proven metabolic pulse — cancer cells can't survive the ROS; healthy cells can

HBOT floods tissues with high-concentration oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Cancer cells, with compromised antioxidant systems, are overwhelmed. Healthy cells, with intact antioxidant defences, handle it without damage. This selective oxidative stress is one of the most biologically elegant aspects of the press-pulse framework.

9

Microplastics and PFAS are damaging your mitochondria — reduce your exposure now

Both classes of compounds accumulate in human tissue and impair mitochondrial membrane function and electron transport chain efficiency. Practical reductions: filter your drinking water (reverse osmosis removes PFAS), replace non-stick cookware with stainless steel or cast iron, reduce plastic food packaging, and avoid heating food in plastic containers.

10

Cut sugar and refined carbs — they are the most direct dietary driver of high GKI and cancer fuel availability

Every gram of sugar and refined carbohydrate raises blood glucose, elevates insulin, and increases GKI. In Seyfried's metabolic framework, the Western diet is essentially a cancer-feeding protocol. The most impactful single dietary change is eliminating added sugars and refined carbohydrates — moving from metabolic chaos (GKI >9) toward the metabolic health zone (GKI 1–3).
