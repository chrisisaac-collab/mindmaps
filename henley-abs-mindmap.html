1The Abs Myth: Fat vs. Muscle
▾
You already have abs+
Everyone has abdominal muscles sitting under the surface. What varies is how much body fat covers them, not whether they exist. Visibility, not existence, is the real variable.
Why fat sits on the midsection+
Bodies tend to store extra fat around the abdomen because it's positioned to protect internal organs — a survival trait. Some people are genetically predisposed to hold less there, but most of us store fat over the abs before almost anywhere else.
Spot reduction is a lie+
Doing endless targeted ab exercises does not pull fat specifically from that area. Fat loss happens across the whole body simultaneously as you sustain a deficit — not region by region based on where you train.
Two levers, not one+
Visible abs require both dropping overall body fat low enough to reveal the muscle, and building the ab muscle itself so it's more prominent once the fat comes down.
The mountain-range analogy+
Think of abs as mountains and body fat as an ocean covering them. Bigger mountains (more ab muscle) poke through a shallower ocean (a moderate fat level) sooner than smaller ones would.
2Training Your Abs: Three Patterns
▾
Why compounds alone aren't enough+
Squats and deadlifts do engage the core, but the same logic that says biceps need direct arm work applies to abs — targeted training drives more growth than incidental engagement from other lifts.
Lower abs progression+
Start with reverse crunches, progress to supported leg raises (focusing on bracing and spinal curl rather than swinging from the hip flexors), then advance to decline hammer raises for a brutal, high-tension finisher.
Upper abs progression+
Standard crunches are the entry point, cable crunches (including a standing variation for gyms without a good setup) build on that, and V-ups round out the upper-ab work with full-range contraction.
Obliques+
Cross-body cable twists and side bends off a 45-degree hip extension target the obliques while also building rotational and lateral spinal strength and control — useful well beyond aesthetics.
Volume guidance+
Train each movement pattern roughly once a week, aiming for around 6–10 direct ab sets total on top of normal compound training — enough to drive growth without crowding out the rest of a program.
3Nutrition: The Deficit Framework
▾
It's mostly the kitchen+
Training builds the muscle, but visibility comes from being in a sustained calorie deficit long enough to strip the stubborn fat that tends to sit over the abs last.
Target rate of loss+
Aim to lose around 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week. For a 70kg person, that's roughly 0.35–0.7kg weekly — achieved through an average daily deficit of a few hundred calories.
Metabolic adaptation+
As body mass drops, calorie needs drop too — and on top of that, the body downregulates energy use further (less fidgeting, less spontaneous movement) the leaner and more deficit-adapted someone becomes.
Protein and hunger management+
Target roughly 1.6–1.8g of protein per kg of bodyweight to protect muscle in a deficit, and lean on high-volume, low-calorie foods like leafy greens and watery fruit to manage hunger.
Adjusting through plateaus+
If scale weight, measurements, and progress photos haven't moved in 2–3 weeks, it signals a plateau — the fix is either tightening consistency or nudging steps up or calories down.

The Abs Myth: Fat vs. Muscle

Everyone has abdominal muscles already — what determines visibility is the layer of body fat sitting on top of them, which the body tends to store around the midsection to protect internal organs. Targeted ab exercises don't pull fat specifically from that area; fat loss happens across the whole body as a deficit is sustained. Getting visible abs therefore comes down to two combined levers: reducing overall body fat low enough to reveal the muscle, and building the ab muscle itself so it's thicker and more prominent once the fat does come down. A bigger "mountain" pokes through a shallower "ocean" sooner.

Training Your Abs

Compound lifts like squats and deadlifts engage the core, but the same logic that applies to any other muscle group applies here — direct, targeted training drives more growth than incidental engagement. The recommended approach covers three movement patterns: lower abs (reverse crunch, progressing to supported leg raises and then decline hammer raises), upper abs (crunches, cable crunches or a standing cable crunch variation, and V-ups), and obliques (cross-body cable twists and side bends off a 45-degree hip extension). The guidance is to hit each pattern about once a week for roughly 6–10 total direct sets, layered on top of normal compound training rather than replacing it.

Nutrition: The Deficit Framework

The nutrition side is described as doing most of the work — sustained calorie deficit is what actually strips the stubborn fat over the abs. A target rate of roughly 0.5–1% of bodyweight lost per week is recommended, achieved through a moderate daily deficit. As weight drops, calorie needs drop too, compounded by metabolic adaptation, where the body further reduces energy expenditure (including subtle drops in daily fidgeting and movement) the longer and deeper the deficit runs. To manage this, the framework calls for protein around 1.6–1.8g per kg of bodyweight, high-volume low-calorie foods to control hunger, and regular check-ins — if weight, measurements, and photos stall for 2–3 weeks, either tighten consistency or adjust steps and calories.

The Hidden Costs

Pursuing visible abs is framed honestly as genuinely difficult, not a 30-day shortcut. Fatigue and hunger intensify as leanness increases, described from firsthand experience as reaching a point of significant depletion. Recovery becomes more important as calories drop — prioritizing sleep, cutting back alcohol, and potentially increasing rest days from one to two or three per week. Socially, the deficit required leaves little flexibility for spontaneous eating occasions, since achieving that level of leanness generally rules out regular indulgent meals.

The Pyramid of Delusion

Most bodies seen in media, on social platforms, or in person fall into a "99%" category, while a much smaller "1%" holds a genuinely elite level of leanness. That top tier is shaped by three factors: genetics (which influences muscle-building potential, appetite regulation, natural activity levels, and fat distribution), devotion (for many, staying lean is effectively their job, allowing a level of focus that isn't realistic for most people's lives), and a subset of "fibbers" who recycle old photos, use undisclosed substances, or rely on flattering lighting, oil, and tan to exaggerate definition. A simple reality check — counting how many people at an actual beach display that level of leanness — is offered as a corrective to the distorted picture social media creates.

Body Image and the Real Payoff

Reflecting on her own experience getting visibly lean, the process was harder and more physically and mentally taxing than expected. Body image often improves in the early stages of fat loss, but that improvement can plateau and reverse the further leanness is pushed, especially alongside constant self-monitoring. Reaching visible abs did not deliver the expected boost in confidence — what genuinely improved body image afterward was reversing course: getting stronger, getting fitter, building more muscle, and eating more food. The closing advice is to get honest about the motivation behind chasing abs before starting, involve a coach if there's a risk of taking it too far, and have a clear plan for returning to maintenance once a fat-loss phase ends.
